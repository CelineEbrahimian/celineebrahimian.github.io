Upper-Body Force-Feedback Exoskeleton

Humanoid teleoperation · Started at Minerva Humanoids, continuing as my FYDP
Mechanical designRapid prototypingMuJoCoMotor-control firmwareCAN busUser testing
In progress · FYDP

What

A wearable upper-body exoskeleton for teleoperating a humanoid robot. It feeds the robot's contact forces back to the operator's arms for more accurate control, and must adjust to fit individual users.

How

  • Researched exoskeleton designs and actuation methods: pulley systems, rotary, linear and pneumatic
  • Rapid-prototyped shoulder motor layouts with bearings and 3D-printed parts to find one that works
  • Validated designs in MuJoCo before printing and testing them in real life
  • Ran user tests on an elbow-motor fixture (CAN bus) to set the motor requirements
  • Owned mechanical, electrical and firmware design; built and wore the first arm

Lessons so far

  • Reference papers with proven results for what you're trying to achieve
  • Nail down requirements early and map them to technical specs to prioritize trade-offs
  • Test with people before choosing hardware
  • Force changes are felt relative to the force already applied
  • For a wearable, fit matters as much as performance

This project is ongoing as my final-year design project. I'll update this section through the term.

What

An autonomous robot that follows a line, finds a target and moves it to a safe zone with a gripper. Limits: 0.22 × 0.22 × 0.25 m and a $300 budget. Scored on speed, weight, and successful runs out of 3.

How

  • Sized and selected the drive motors
  • C++ motor firmware: PWM, encoder feedback, closed-loop speed control
  • Vision, control and motor loops on separate threads
  • Swapped an unreliable IMU for encoder-based odometry
  • Python logging tool to tune 40+ parameters
  • Drive-system parts in SolidWorks

Lessons

  • Start firmware early
  • Simple beats complex: encoders beat the IMU
  • Real dynamics need empirical tuning
  • Most bugs came from hardware × firmware interactions

Two-Axis Gantry Embedded Control System

Embedded control & safety · STM32 · lead-screw axes
CSTM32ADCInterrupts & timersLimit-switch safetyOscilloscope

What

Control a lead-screw two-axis gantry with two potentiometers on an STM32, with a limit-switch safety layer. The target must be repeatable to within 1 cm.

How

  • Key decision: each pot maps to an X–Y position, not direction/speed, for better accuracy and repeatability
  • Homing sequence at start-up finds each axis's full range, so ADC readings map to accurate positions
  • Limit switches as a safety layer: if one is hit, the axis backs off slightly
  • Interrupt- and timer-based motion control; tested with UART logs and an oscilloscope

Lessons

  • Home first: knowing the full range makes every reading meaningful
  • Build fail-safes in from day one
  • Interrupts beat polling for real-time control

What

A robot that identifies plant types by color and gives each one its own water dose, with basic safety and fault handling.

How

  • Custom EV3 chassis with a winch-driven spray mechanism
  • Color sensing to classify each plant and choose travel direction
  • Dosing algorithm linking classification to motion and actuation
  • Fault stops and safety logic written in C/C++

Lessons
